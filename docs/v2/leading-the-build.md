# Leading the build

For whoever leads the v2 build next, a person or an agent that takes it over part way. [The plan](plan.md) says what is built and why; section 9 of it says how the build is run, and section 15 gives the order of work and the owner's calendar. This says how that has worked in practice, and what each lesson cost, so that it is paid for once.

## If you are starting now

The owner starts a new lead with a message of their own, such as: "Take over as lead of the eva-md v2 build. Read AGENTS.md and docs/v2/leading-the-build.md, and carry on from 'Where the build stands'." A session is the lead's once the owner has said so in it; until then it is any other agent under `AGENTS.md`, and neither commits nor pushes. The owner's words are your leave, `AGENTS.md` records its limits, and they are also what a wave's orders quote (`authority`, below). Then:

1. Read `AGENTS.md`, this file in full, and the index, [cards/README.md](cards/README.md).
2. Read "Where the build stands", below. It was true when it was last pushed; `git log -5 --format='%h %cd %s' -- docs/v2/leading-the-build.md docs/v2/cards/README.md` says when that was.
3. Nothing that ran in the last lead's session is running now, and its agents' reports are gone with it. A card the index calls building or in review is waiting for you.
4. Fetch the safety copy, `git fetch origin <branch>`, with the branch "Where the build stands" names. `git log --grep='^Safety copy' --format='%h %cd%n%b' FETCH_HEAD` lists, newest first, each branch that was copied and its tip. For a package you take up, make its branch again at its newest tip, `git branch pkg/<id> <tip>`, and give it to a builder: the builder's order in [wave.js](wave.js) says how to carry on from a branch that exists, and a builder may judge it not worth keeping and start from the card. A branch that looks finished is still reviewed and verified again before it is merged, since no report vouches for it. A package with no branch in the copy is built from its card.
5. Read CI for the head of `main`, as step 5 of "How a package goes from card to main" says, and mend what is red before anything else.
6. Tell the owner, in plain words, what you found and what you are starting.

## Always ready to hand over

The owner's rule, given on 2026-11-13: the lead works as if the build were handed to someone else at any moment, and answers for it. A session can end and a machine can restart without warning, and the next lead has only the repository and the remote. So, at every change of state, and not at the end of a day:

- the index says what is building, in review, merged or owed, in the same push as the change;
- "Where the build stands", below, says what is running now, what comes next and what is kept beside `main`, and is rewritten when any of that changes;
- a ruling the lead makes is written into the card it concerns when it is made, not at the merge;
- every branch that holds work not on `main` has its tip in the safety copy (lesson 18). While a wave runs, [safety-copy.sh](safety-copy.sh) renews it every 15 minutes and lists each branch and its tip in the copy's message; when a builder is cut off, work it left uncommitted is committed on its branch first (lesson 25);
- a lesson is written here when it is learned.

The test is a reader with nothing but a fresh clone: can it say what to do next, and find everything it needs?

## Where the state is

- **The plan** governs. A card that differs from it is wrong and is fixed.
- **The cards and their index**, [cards/README.md](cards/README.md): one card for each package, and for each its state (ready, building, in review, merged, or what it waits for). Keep the index true at every change of state: mark a card `building` in the push that starts its builder, `in review` when its reviewers start, and `merged` at the merge. It is the only list of where the build stands.
- **`main`'s history.** Each package is one commit whose message says what it gives and what its reviewers found.
- **CI**, for each commit: the tests on macOS, which is the run of record; the latency gate in a job of its own; the checks on Linux.
- **Not in the repository:** the lead's working notes, and the branches of packages still being built, which exist only on the machine that builds them. A session that ends takes both with it. So what must outlive a session is written into a card, the index or this file, and a package is merged as soon as it is sound. One that is not merged when a session ends is built again from its card. On that machine `git branch --list 'pkg/*'` and `git worktree list` show what is in progress, but a squash merge leaves its branch behind, so a branch alone proves nothing.

## How a package goes from card to main

1. **The card first, and pushed.** A builder starts in a worktree cut from `main` as the remote has it. A card that is only on the lead's machine is not there.
2. **A builder** makes a branch `pkg/<id>`, commits as it goes, runs `scripts/linux-check.sh`, and reports against the card's "Accepted when" list, item by item, with what shows each.
3. **Reviewers** get the card and the diff, and are told to break it. Approval is worth little; a defect with its evidence is worth a lot. One reviewer is enough for a package that changes nothing v1 does. Two for a hook, for redaction, for the record and the store, for the pause switch, and for anything that reads a person's chats or puts text in one (plan section 9): one looks at the contract and whether each test can fail, the other at what could be kept, printed or shown that should not be. A rule that is a matter of chance, as the latency gate's is, gets a reviewer who measures it by simulation with a harness of their own.
4. **A fixer** answers the reviews on a branch `pkg/<id>-r1`: every blocker and major is fixed with a test, and an issue it is sure is wrong is declined with its evidence.
5. **The lead** reads the reports, checks the diff's paths against the card's "Owns and may touch" with `git diff --stat main...pkg/<id>-r1` and asks for every path outside it to be named in the report, runs the quick document and script tests `AGENTS.md` names on the result, squashes the branch into one commit on `main`, and writes its message: what it gives, why, and what the reviewers found, with `Card: <ID>` on a line of its own straight after the subject, which CI's `guards` job reads. That commit holds the package's files only, and its card is already on `main` with a `paths` block that covers them; a path the block lacks is added first, in a commit of the lead's. In a second commit, with no such line, the lead writes the card's notes and the lines of `AGENTS.md` and `README.md` the card kept back, and marks the card merged in the index. `go run ./cmd/eva-eval paths origin/main HEAD` says before the push what the job will say after it. The lead pushes, reads CI for that commit, and fixes a failure forward at once. CI is read with `gh run list --commit <sha>` and `gh run view <id> --log-failed` where gh is there, or with the session's own GitHub tools. A green run says nothing of the latency gate, whose step is advisory: its verdict is in that step's log or in the run's `latency` artifact, and the verdict of every run that counts goes into the table in [baselines/README.md](baselines/README.md).

The owner's leave for all of this, and its limits, are in `AGENTS.md` ("During the v2 build the owner has given standing leave"). A release is never the lead's to decide: no tag, and no release workflow unless the owner asks for it in so many words (see the last section). Within the build only the lead pushes; builders, reviewers and fixers commit on their own branches.

## What a builder or a reviewer is told, every time

An agent that starts a task knows only what its order says. Each order carries:

- **Why the task is the owner's.** An agent that sees only the owner's latest words may take them for the whole task and refuse the rest. The order quotes the owner's leave, says which of their messages are settled constraints and not the task, and says that building or reviewing this package is what was asked.
- **Start from `main` as it is now:** fetch it, and `git merge --ff-only origin/main`, which never discards anything; a branch with commits of its own rebases on it instead. A fix to a script reaches a builder only when its branch holds it: a builder on an older branch keeps running the old script. After pushing one, tell every running builder to rebase on `origin/main` before its next check.
- **The limits:** work only in your worktree; never push, tag or start a release; never run setup, install, update or the like against the real home, and never read the real home, `~/.claude` or `~/.codex` with a script of your own; `internal/redact/baseline` is never edited; no new module; made-up values in fixtures.
- **Commit at every working state**, on its own branch, with plain messages that name no model or assistant. An agent's commits carry no trailer; the lead's carry the one its session gives.
- **The checks:** `scripts/linux-check.sh`, and `scripts/linux-gate.sh` for anything a hook runs; what a failure of the benchmark under load looks like, and that code is never changed to chase it.
- **A folder of your own for scratch files.** Never write into another agent's.
- **For a reviewer:** take no claim of the builder's on trust; run the checks yourself; change one line and see whether a test notices.

An order can start from this, with the package's own words put in:

> The owner of this repository asked for the v2 build and gave standing leave for it (`AGENTS.md`, "During the v2 build the owner has given standing leave"). Building <id> from `docs/v2/cards/<id>.md` is that request; the owner's other messages, about releases or live runs, are settled limits, not your task. Run `git fetch origin main`, then `git merge --ff-only origin/main`; if it refuses because your branch has commits of its own, rebase on `origin/main`, and never `git reset --hard` over changes `git status` shows. Work on branch `pkg/<id>`, commit at every working state, and keep scratch files only in <folder>. Run `scripts/linux-check.sh`; a benchmark failure under load is run again when the machine is quiet, never chased in code. Work only in your worktree; never run setup, install, update or the like against the real home; never edit `internal/redact/baseline`; add no module; fixtures hold made-up values. For anything a hook runs, also run `scripts/linux-gate.sh`. Never push, tag or start a release. Report against the card's "Accepted when", item by item. (For a reviewer: take no claim on trust, run the checks yourself, and change one line to see whether a test notices.)

## The wave script

[wave.js](wave.js) is the script the lead has run each wave with. It holds the orders above in full, so a lead with another way of running agents takes the orders from it. The harness it is written for is the lead's session's own workflow tool, which gives a script `args`, `agent`, `parallel`, `pipeline` and `log`; the script's path and its arguments are passed to that tool, whose own description says how a run is started, where its journal is and how a run is resumed. For each package the script runs a builder, a reviewer for each lens its entry names, a fixer when a reviewer found anything or the builder's check did not pass, and then, with `verify: true`, verifications and fixes in turn, ending on a verification (lesson 19).

The lenses are `general`, `contract`, `privacy`, `protection`, and `rule` for a rule of chance or of counting, which its reviewer measures by simulation (step 3 above); the script refuses any other name. A card's `Reviewers:` line gives them. One reviewer is `['contract']`, or `['general']` where there is no contract to hold the package to. Two are `['contract', 'privacy']`, with `protection` in place of `privacy` for a package that reads a lock or a verified mark. What the card says its second reviewer hunts for goes into the package's `note`.

It returns one entry for each package. `build`, `reviews`, `fix` and `rounds` hold the agents' reports; `final` is the report of the last branch that was finished (`final.branch`, `final.sha`); `verified` is `true` when the last round was a verification that found no blocker and no major, and `false` when it found one or a round did not finish. The lead merges an entry whose `verified` is `true`. An entry with no `verified` at all is merged only when it has no `fix`, `reviews` holds a report for every lens asked, and no report has an issue: a reviewer that was cut off is left out of `reviews` without a word, and an entry whose reviews hold issues had no `verify: true` or a fixer that did not finish. `final.words_for_the_lead`, `final.outside_card` and `final.open_questions` are what the lead writes into `AGENTS.md`, writes cards for and rules on at the merge.

Its arguments are `scratch`, the folder under which each agent makes a scratch folder of its own; `agent`, the options the harness gives every agent besides its worktree, such as its model and effort, which the owner sets in the lead's session and which are not kept in this repository, so that a new lead asks the owner for them, and without them every agent runs on the harness's defaults; `authority`, the owner's own words on the build, which are not kept here either, and without which an order rests on what `AGENTS.md` records; `rounds`, the most verifications a package gets (3); and `packages`, each with its `id`, its `lenses`, a `note` from the lead and `verify: true`. `verify_note` holds the lead's words for the verifiers after the first, and for the first too with `verify_note_first` or a `round_offset`: it is how a verifier is told to make a list whole or to check a construction (lessons 19 and 22).

A wave that was cut off is not run again from the start. A stage that finished is given back to the script: `build`, `reviews` and `fix` take the reports of the agents that finished, which are in the run's journal, and that stage is skipped. `more_lenses` asks for the reviews still owed; `round_offset`, the number of fix rounds already made, makes the fix branches go on from the last round's number; and `fix_branch` names the branch a cut-off fixer of the first round left, from which the next fixer carries on. The harness can also resume a run by its id, replaying the agents that finished, but only while the script and its arguments are unchanged: after any change, give the finished stages instead. A journal ends with its session, so a new session starts again from branches and cards, not from reports.

The script sets no limit of its own: it starts every package it is given at once, and a package's reviewers together. So the lead sets a wave's size by the packages it passes, two or three a wave, and keeps all the waves that run to four or five agents at once on four CPUs (lessons 3, 13 and 20).

No test reads `wave.js` or [safety-copy.sh](safety-copy.sh): the wording test reads only the `.md` files under `docs/`. A change to either is read by eye against `AGENTS.md`'s rule that the repository is public, and the owner's words, the `agent` options and a note that names a machine stay in the arguments, never in the file.

## What the first waves taught

Each of these happened. Items 3 to 8 and 11 are now rules in `AGENTS.md` or in a script; item 10 is in the cards' README; items 1, 2, 9 and 12 are held by the steps and orders above, so they hold only as long as each order carries them; the later ones are the lead's to remember.

1. **Three builders started from a commit that lacked their cards**, because the cards were committed and not yet pushed. Push the cards, then start builders.
2. **Builders refused to build.** They read the owner's last message, which was about releases, as the whole task. Every order now says why its task is the owner's.
3. **The disk filled, and the machine was restarted.** Go's build cache keeps each package under the folder it was built in, every check ran in a snapshot with a folder of its own, and the cache reached 23 GB in a day. `linux-check.sh` now keeps a run's cache with its snapshot and removes both, and refuses to start unless 2,500 MB are free plus 2,000 MB for each check already running; `scripts/linux-space.sh` shows and frees the rest. Before starting a wave, run `scripts/linux-space.sh`, start no more builders than (free MB − 500) / 2,000, since each may run a check at once, and prune first if that is fewer than you need. A builder on a branch from before that fix still fills the old shared cache: have it rebase, and prune between waves until all have.
4. **The restart lost a fixer three hours into its work.** Its commits were there; its report and its unfinished proof were not. What saved the proof was that it could be started again where it stopped. Long jobs are written that way, and agents commit as they go.
5. **A test passed on Linux and failed on macOS**, where a temporary folder is reached through an alias. No builder can run macOS, so tests are written for it, and CI is read after every push. It happened a second time, with the capture audit: a rule that compared a folder with `/tmp` was right on Linux and wrong on macOS, where `/tmp` is a link and a folder that is gone cannot be resolved. A test of such a rule names its root through a link, so that Linux shows what macOS would.
6. **A new script turned CI red.** A test reads every `go build` line of every script, and it read a closing parenthesis as part of the package's name. The tests that read scripts, workflows and documents take seconds: run them before pushing one.
7. **Editing a script while it ran broke that run** part way through.
8. **Timing under load proves nothing.** With several agents testing, the benchmark failed on code that was sound, and the latency gate said "not judged". In CI the gate first ran after the race tests and could not judge; it has a job of its own now.
9. **Twenty clean runs did not show what a simulation did.** The gate passed an unchanged binary 19 times of 20 on a quiet machine. A reviewer who fed its rule made-up samples under load found that it called a real slowdown of a fifth "not judged" two times in three, and failed an unchanged binary under some patterns of load. A rule of chance is reviewed by simulation, not only by running it.
10. **Two packages built at once wanted the same file.** Where that could be seen coming, a small registry removed it: commands, status rows and switches each register from a file of their own, and so do the evaluation command's instruments. Where it could not, as with one pinned line of text, the lead joins the two at the merge.
11. **A reviewer overwrote a builder's files** in a scratch folder they shared. Each agent now has its own.
12. **A prompt for the real install is reviewed like code.** The first one, [field-report-prompt.md](field-report-prompt.md), asked for three commands that failed on that machine. The draft of the second would have let model names, page paths and project names into its report, until a reviewer went through it command by command. Such a prompt is reviewed for privacy and for whether every command in it exists. The second is [field-report-2-prompt.md](field-report-2-prompt.md), with the message that goes with it in [field-report-2-message.md](field-report-2-message.md): before the owner takes it there, each command it names is run with the release stand-in's binary (the release's code, built with the tests' `evamd_testlimits` tag so that it runs on Linux), in a home of its own, never the real home.
13. **Builders waited hours for a quiet machine.** Six agents on four CPUs kept the load at eight times the CPUs. The latency gate said "not judged", the rule told each builder to run it again when the machine was quiet, and it never was, so the packages on the way to a release stood still. A builder now waits half an hour at most and reports the load; the lead reads CI's `latency` job on the merge. And a wave is no larger than the machine: the next one starts when the last one's checks are over, and the packages a release waits for go first.
14. **A review found a fault in a package already merged.** The codec's reviewers found nine ways to write a lock that the format checker, merged before it, read as no lock. A finding outside a package's paths is not fixed in that package: the builder reports it, and the lead writes a card for it, as ST-01b is for this one.
15. **An agent tried its script on the build machine's own host folders.** Asked to write a reader of the hosts' session files, a writer ran it over the real `~/.claude` of the machine it worked on, which holds the record of real sessions. It printed only counts, and none of it reached the repository, but the rule that tests never touch the real machine had not been said to an agent that was writing no test. Every order now says it: nothing an agent writes or runs reads the real home, `~/.claude` or `~/.codex`; a made-up home under its own scratch folder is the only test data.
16. **A file that has an agent write its own scripts over a person's records leaked in three trials running.** It was meant to settle host facts on the real install with no sitting. Each trial, in a made-up home with markers planted in every value and key, found new leaks, also after the rule became "print only names on a fixed list": a path in a traceback, a version's suffix, a word in another script, digits that were not ASCII. Reading a person's records belongs in tested code with fixed lists (card HF-01), as the report and the capture audit are, and a prompt for the real install only runs such commands and copies their numbers. The trials were what showed it: a privacy review alone had passed the first version with fixes.
17. **A list of failures carries no times.** A wave reported ten agents failed on a usage limit when it ended, two hours after they had failed and after the limit had been lifted. The lead read it as a limit in force and told the owner the build had stopped. Before acting on a failure a workflow reports, read the last line of a failed agent's transcript for its time, and look at whether other agents are answering now.
18. **Built branches live on one machine until they merge.** A dozen packages, built and waiting for a review or for the release, existed only as local branches on a cloud machine that nothing promises to keep. The lead now keeps their tips as the parents of one commit, with `main`'s tree, on the session's own branch on the remote: it is no branch to merge, and a tip is fetched from it by its id. Renew it when a wave ends, with the last such commit as a parent, so that the push is a fast-forward.
19. **A wave ended on a fix nobody had checked.** The wave's script gave a package one verification and, when that found a fault, one more fix, and stopped there. One package came back three times with its last fix unread, and each verification found one more fault of the same kind: one more sort of file that names another chat and was not counted with it. A wave now ends on a verification: verify, fix, verify, for up to three rounds, and a branch whose last round was a fix is reported as not verified. When two verifications running find the same kind of fault, the next verifier is told to make the list of that kind whole, not to find one more.
20. **Too many teams at once spent the usage allowance.** With four or five workflows running, the agents reached their five-hour limit twice in one day, and everything in flight was cut off, some of it part way through a check. Fewer agents at a time finish more. Run a few, in the order of what the owner waits for: what a release or a sitting needs, then the reviews of what is built, then new builds; start the next when one ends. A round of judges that reads a great deal, as the contract matrix's twelve rulings did, spends as much as a wave of builds: count it as one.
21. **A push to `main` delays a release that waits to be cut.** Cut a release checks that CI is green for the head of `main`, a push starts about 35 minutes of CI, and the tag is named for the day of that head's commit. So when the owner is told a release is ready, the lead names the commit, and keeps what would follow on a local branch, with a copy on the remote beside the built branches. The owner decides what the release takes. For the counters release, then expected as 2026.11.12 and cut as 2026.11.13, they wanted what was still coming: the lead then pushes it to `main` as it passes its checks, and tells the owner again, with the new tag's name, when CI is green on a head that holds it.
22. **A bar no sample can meet kept a package in review for nine rounds.** LG-12's verifiers were told that any widening of a redaction exception which passed the oracle was a major fault. The far side of an exception is an open set, the oracle's near cases sample it, and six verifiers in a row each found one more widening that no case met. The lead then ruled what the package must hold: every change to the package's own code fails some test, the oracle for what its cases sample and a fingerprint of the source for the rest. The fingerprint then took three rounds, since it walked from a list of roots and twice missed code; it now holds every declaration, and the verifier checked how it is built, by parsing the package, in place of looking for one more gap. When verifications keep finding one more of a kind, the lead changes what is asked: make the property hold by construction, and have the verifier check the construction.
23. **"Not judged" is where the reading of latency starts.** By its second rule the gate has judged one of its first eighteen runs on CI's runner (runs 110 to 127). For a merge that changes what a hook runs, the lead reads the run's `latency` artifact, which holds the new binary's own figures on macOS against their targets, and runs `scripts/linux-gate.sh` on the commit before with nothing else running: that one judges. When a figure misses its target on a commit that should not have moved it, `go list -deps ./cmd/eva-md` says whether any package the binary holds changed at all.
24. **A red benchmark straight after a hook change is measured before it is put down to load.** CI's Linux job counted 60 background starts for 62 on a push made straight after the hook's handler list merged. Two agents ran the old binary and the new side by side over 9,760 hooks: every lost start had a hook past its limit, and the old binary lost more than the new (the figures are the two agents' reports, kept in no card or commit). Only then was the job run again. EV-16c is the card that makes the count allow for it.
25. **A cut-off agent leaves work that is not committed.** A usage limit or a restart stops an agent between commits. Before a wave is started again, `git -C <worktree> status` for each of its worktrees: real work is committed on the agent's branch as work in progress, a change an agent made only to see a test fail is put back, and the next agent is given that branch to carry on from.
26. **The owner's words about a release were read two ways.** Asked for a complete stop of the background work before the release, the lead stopped three waves at once; the owner had meant that they should finish first, and they were started again. When the owner's words on a release can be read two ways, the lead says which reading it takes and what that leaves out of the release, before it acts.

## What is the lead's alone

- The cards, the index and this file.
- `AGENTS.md` and `README.md`, beyond the lines a card names for its builder.
- The merge: the commit on `main`, its message, and CI's result after it.
- The stored latency baseline, taken from a pass of CI's own job, and the count of passes toward the twenty with no fail between them that make the gate blocking, kept in [baselines/README.md](baselines/README.md). A change to the gate's rule starts that count again.

## Before the owner is told a release is ready

- Every package the release is to hold is merged. Each that touches a hook has its latency comparison reported, as lesson 23 says.
- A prompt the owner takes to the real install is finished and reviewed as lesson 12 says.
- CI is green for the head of `main` on both test jobs, and `scripts/release.sh --plan` passes and names the tag.
- Nothing is running that would push to `main` (lesson 21).

Then the lead tells the owner it is ready, with the commit and the tag. The owner cuts it, or tells the lead in so many words to start the release workflow, whose run then waits for the owner's approval. The lead never cuts one unasked.
